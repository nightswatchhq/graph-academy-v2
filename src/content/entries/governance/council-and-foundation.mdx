Two bodies, two jobs. Confusing them makes a great deal of ecosystem discussion
unreadable.

## The Council.

The Graph Council is a six-of-ten multisig that governs the protocol. It balances five
stakeholder groups: indexers, active token holders, the initial team, users, and
technical domain experts.

Two features of that design are worth noticing.

**It is a multisig, not a token vote.** Protocol decisions are not made by whoever
holds the most GRT. This is a deliberate choice with a real trade-off: it avoids
plutocracy and concentrates authority in ten seats.

**The seats represent groups with opposed interests.** Indexers and users want
different things about query pricing. Token holders and the team want different things
about issuance. Requiring six of ten across those groups means a change has to be
tolerable to more than one constituency.

<Model>
The Council is designed so that no single interest group can pass a change alone, and
so that the people voting are identifiable and accountable. It buys accountability at
the cost of decentralisation, and that trade is worth understanding rather than
assuming either way.
</Model>

## The Foundation.

The Graph Foundation is a separate body. It does not vote on protocol changes. It holds
and deploys funds: grants, ecosystem support, and work that needs paying for.

Its main visible output is the grants programme, which runs in waves supporting
protocol development, tooling, integrations, community work and research. If you are
building something for this ecosystem, that is the door.

<Warn title="Do not conflate them">
The Council approves protocol changes. The Foundation deploys funding. A grant is not
protocol approval, and a Council vote does not release money.

The Innovation Allocation is a good example of both being involved and each doing its
own job: the Council voted to redirect
<Param name="innovation_allocation_pct" /> of protocol issuance, and the Foundation
treasury is what receives it.
</Warn>

## On naming individuals.

This site deliberately does not name current officeholders, and the omission is
considered rather than lazy.

People move. A learning page that names a Foundation director or a Council member is
correct for a while and then quietly wrong, and the reader has no way to tell which
state it is in. Structures change more slowly than the people occupying them, so this
page teaches the structure and links to the source for the current membership.

That is a general principle worth borrowing: in evergreen material, describe the role,
link for the occupant.

## What you can actually do.

Governance here is more open to participation than it first appears:

- **Read the GIP repository.** Nothing is required to do this and it is the best
  documentation available.
- **Comment on the forum.** GIP discussion is public and substantive comments from
  people running infrastructure carry weight, because Council members are not
  operating everything themselves.
- **Write a GIP.** The process is permissionless. The bar is that it must be a real
  argument.
- **Apply for a grant.** If you are building tooling the ecosystem needs, that is what
  the programme is for.
- **Watch the Council votes.** They are on Snapshot and they are public.

The realistic path to influence for most people is the second one. Being the person who
points out a mechanism flaw during discussion is worth more than any amount of
commentary afterwards.
