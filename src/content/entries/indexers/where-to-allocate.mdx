You have stake that is not allocated, and a few hundred deployments you could put it
on. Each of them pays indexing rewards, and each is already shared with somebody. The
question is where the stake earns most, and the answer is less obvious than sorting by
APR.

This lesson explains the reasoning, then how the
[Lodestar](https://www.lodestar-dashboard.com/indexers) allocation simulator on an
indexer's Plan tab applies it, and then what its estimate does not know.

## What a deployment pays.

Indexing rewards come from issuance paid to the Rewards Manager, currently
<Param name="subgraph_service_issuance_per_block" />. That issuance is split across
deployments by curation signal, and a deployment's share is split across the
allocations on it by allocated stake. The official docs put it as "rewards are
distributed proportionally based on curation signal and the amount of GRT allocated to
each subgraph deployment."

So for one deployment, over a year:

```text
K = annual issuance × deployment signal ÷ network signal
your rewards = K × y ÷ (O + y)

  K  the deployment's annual reward pool
  y  your stake on it
  O  everyone else's stake on it
```

Your rewards depend on other people's allocations, which is the fact everything else in
this lesson follows from.

## The first GRT and the next one.

What you want to know about a deployment is what one more GRT would earn there. The
formula gives it directly:

```text
marginal reward = K × O ÷ (O + y)²
```

With nothing on it yet (`y` = 0), that is `K ÷ O`: the deployment's reward pool over
the stake already on it. This is why signal over stake is the column worth sorting by.
Each GRT you add earns less than the one before, because you are increasingly competing
with yourself for the same pool.

<Model>
Average APR is the wrong guide. A deployment where you already hold a large share can
show a handsome APR on your existing stake while the next GRT earns very little there.
Allocation decisions are made at the margin, and the margin is where the average hides
the answer.
</Model>

## Filling the level.

If each deployment's marginal reward falls as you add stake, the best split is the one
where no GRT could be moved somewhere it earns more. That means every deployment you
allocate to ends at the same marginal reward, and a deployment whose first GRT earns
less than that level gets nothing. It is called water-filling, after the picture: pour
stake in and it finds the lowest level first.

For the smooth case there is a closed form. For some level `λ`, each deployment gets
`√(K × O ÷ λ) − O`, or nothing if that is negative, and `λ` is chosen so the amounts
add up to your stake.

Two things break that formula in practice.

**Deployments nobody serves.** With `O` = 0, the first GRT takes the whole pool and
every GRT after it earns nothing. The curve is not smooth at zero, and the square-root
formula cannot see it.

**A smallest sensible allocation.** Opening a 3 GRT allocation is technically possible
and practically silly, so a real plan has a minimum size, and a minimum is a jump.

## How the simulator does it.

The simulator works greedily instead. It hands out the amount you want to place a
chunk at a time, a chunk being a four-hundredth of the amount, each to the deployment
where that chunk adds the most yearly reward. A deployment it has not yet used can only
be opened with at least the minimum allocation, so an opening step may be larger than a
chunk, and it is judged on what it earns as a whole. That handles both of the cases
above. On the smooth part it tends towards the equal level the closed form describes,
which is why, in the example below, two deployments end within a whisker of each other.
It is a close approximation rather than a guarantee.

The inputs, all shown on the Plan tab:

- **Candidates.** The 200 deployments with the most signal, plus any you are already
  allocated to. Deployments with rewards denied are left out.
- **Filters.** A minimum signal, an optional cap per deployment, chains to exclude,
  deployments to skip, and deployments to pin. The cap stops the plan adding stake; it
  does not shrink an allocation already above it. Pins are funded first, with the
  minimum allocation or the cap if that is lower, provided the amount stretches that
  far. A pinned deployment that is skipped or denied is still left out.
- **Amount.** By default, your available stake.
- **Count current allocations.** On, your open allocations stay and the amount goes on
  top. Off, the plan starts from nothing, with the amount including what is allocated
  today, and may close things.
- **Issuance and signal.** The Rewards Manager's current issuance and the network's
  total signal, refreshed every few minutes.

The output is a table of current against proposed stake per deployment, the estimated
rewards a day and APR, and the `graph indexer actions queue` lines that move your open
allocations to the plan. A deployment whose total changes gets its largest allocation
resized, usually. The lines only queue actions: nothing runs until they are approved,
as the [indexer CLI documentation](https://thegraph.com/docs/en/indexing/overview/#indexer-management-using-indexer-cli)
describes.

The plan can place less than you asked for. If every remaining candidate is capped,
filtered out, or has no room for another valid step, the rest stays unplaced, and the
Plan tab shows what was actually placed.

## A worked example.

Three deployments, run through the simulator's own code. The figures are illustrative,
chosen so the pools are round.

| | Pool `K` a year | Others' stake `O` | First GRT earns |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50,000 GRT | 5,000,000 GRT | 1.0% |
| B | 10,000 GRT | 200,000 GRT | 5.0% |
| C | 1,000 GRT | none | the whole pool |

{/* param-lint-allow: 10,000 GRT (the simulator's default minimum allocation, not a protocol parameter) */}
{/* param-lint-allow: 100,000 GRT (an illustrative budget, not the minimum self-stake) */}
With 100,000 GRT and a minimum allocation of 10,000 GRT:

| | Proposed | Rewards a year | APR |
|---|---|---|---|
| B | 90,000 GRT | 3,103 GRT | 3.45% |
| C | 10,000 GRT | 1,000 GRT | 10.00% |

A gets nothing. After 90,000 GRT on B, the next GRT there still earns about 2.4%, well
above the 1.0% that A's first GRT would.

With 400,000 GRT:

| | Proposed | Rewards a year | APR |
|---|---|---|---|
| B | 259,000 GRT | 5,643 GRT | 2.18% |
| A | 131,000 GRT | 1,277 GRT | 0.97% |
| C | 10,000 GRT | 1,000 GRT | 10.00% |

Now B's marginal reward has fallen far enough that A is worth opening, and the two end
at the same marginal rate, about 0.95%. Their average APRs differ by more than double,
which is the Model above in numbers.

<Reveal question="C has the best APR by a distance. Why does it get only the minimum?">
Because on a deployment nobody else serves, you already take the whole pool with the
first allocation. Every GRT past the minimum earns nothing more there, so the next chunk
goes to a deployment where it adds something. The 10.00% is an average over the smallest allocation the plan
allows, and it lasts exactly until another indexer allocates to C.
</Reveal>

## What the estimate does not know.

The figures are indexing rewards before anybody's cut, on the assumption that nothing
else moves. Each of these can make the plan wrong.

- **Other indexers move.** `O` is today's figure. An unserved deployment like C is
  precisely the one somebody else is likely to join.
- **Your cut.** Rewards are split between you and your delegators by your reward cut.
  The APR shown is on the whole allocation, before that split.
- **Query fees.** Not in the model. A deployment with real query volume is worth more
  than its rewards alone.
- **Cost and time to sync.** Rewards need valid POIs, and a deployment you cannot sync
  or keep synced earns nothing. Chain, size and your hardware do not appear anywhere in
  the formula.
- **Eligibility.** Denied deployments are excluded, but whether your indexer qualifies
  for rewards at all is a separate gate. See
  [rewards eligibility](/indexers/rewards-eligibility/).
- **Gas and allocation count.** Executing the plan costs gas. The agent batches
  compatible actions into multicalls, but the simulator neither estimates that cost
  against a small gain nor caps how many allocations it opens.
  [allocation-optimizer](https://github.com/graphprotocol/allocation-optimizer), the
  optimiser the official docs recommend, does both.
- **The candidate list.** Only the 200 most signalled deployments and your own are
  considered.

## Before you paste the commands

- Look at every proposed allocation on a deployment with no other indexer, and decide
  whether you would still want it once someone joins.
- Check you can sync each new deployment, on that chain, in reasonable time.
- Read the close lines. With current allocations not counted, the plan can close
  allocations you meant to keep.
- Compare the plan's daily estimate with what your current allocations earn by the same
  formula. The Plan tab shows both. If the gain is small, the transactions may not be
  worth it.
- Remember that the queue still needs approving, and approve it deliberately.
