This entry does not tell you how to install anything. Installation instructions
belong in the official documentation, where they are maintained by people who change
them when the software changes.

What this covers is the shape of the system, so that when something breaks you have
a model of what could be broken.

## The components and what they need.

**graph-node** needs chain access, Postgres, and more disk and memory than you first
budgeted. It is the component that does the actual indexing, and it is where most
resource problems originate. Every subgraph it indexes is a schema in Postgres that
grows without bound.

**Postgres** is not an afterthought here. Query latency, indexing throughput and your
backup story all run through it. Treat it as a first-class database with a real
operator, because that is what it is.

**Chain access** is the cost that surprises people. Indexing history means reading
history, which means archive access. Run your own and you are operating another
substantial piece of infrastructure. Buy it and it is a recurring bill that scales
with what you index.

**indexer-agent** needs an operator key, network access, and your indexing rules. It
takes the decisions.

**indexer-service-rs** is the public face. It is stateless, so it scales
horizontally, and it needs to be reachable and fast.

**indexer-tap-agent** runs as a single instance and turns receipts into redeemable
vouchers. If it is down, you serve queries for free.

## Operator keys and stake.

Your stake sits in one wallet. The day-to-day actions, opening allocations,
submitting POIs, redeeming vouchers, are performed by an operator key that is
authorised to act for you without being able to move the stake.

Use it. Separating the key that runs the machine from the key that holds the capital
is the single highest-value operational decision available to you, and it costs one
transaction to set up.

## Which failures are loud, and which are silent.

The loud ones look after themselves. A process that dies gets restarted, and disk
filling triggers whatever you already have.

The silent ones are the expensive category, because in every case the system appears
healthy and the money stops:

| Failure | What you see | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| POI age past the staleness limit | Everything running | The allocation stops earning |
| Subgraph failed on a contract upgrade | One deployment not syncing | That allocation goes stale in a month |
| tap-agent stopped | Queries served normally | Queries served for free |
| RAV redemption reverting | Vouchers accumulating | Earned revenue never lands |
| Allocated to unsignalled subgraphs | Healthy indexing | No indexing rewards |
| Cost model priced above the field | Low query volume | No query fees, and it looks like low demand |

Note what they have in common. Not one is a systems failure. Every one is an economic
failure that a systems dashboard reports as green.

<Model>
Monitor the money, not just the machine. Unaggregated receipt value, age of oldest
unredeemed RAV, POI age per allocation, and query volume against the field. Those are
the indexing metrics. CPU is a supporting act.
</Model>

## Shards, and the wall at the second database.

One Postgres eventually stops being the answer, because every deployment you index
grows without bound and you cannot delete your way out of it. graph-node's answer is
**shards**: several Postgres databases, with each deployment living entirely inside
one of them.

That makes shard placement a decision rather than a detail, and the thing worth
knowing before you make it is what changing your mind later costs. There are two
answers and the gap between them is large.

**Moving a deployment between shards of the same database** is supported.
`graphman copy` does it, and it is the tool for this job rather than a workaround.

**Moving to a different database entirely** is a different problem, and
`graphman copy` does not cover it. This is the boundary operators meet unprepared,
because nothing signposts it: the command that solved the first case simply does not
address the second. Decommissioning a server, or moving between hosting providers,
lands you here.

<Warn title="This path is real and it is not in the documentation">
`docs/graphman.md` in graph-node documents Info, Remove, Unassign, Unused Record,
Unused Remove, Drop, Check Blocks and Chain Call Cache Remove. It does not document
`copy`, which is why an operator asking this question gets pointed to Discord rather
than to a page.

A pull request adding it was open and unmerged when this was last checked on
2026-08-31, so check whether the documentation has caught up before assuming it has
not. `graphman dump` and `restore` also exist, are recent, are labelled experimental,
and work one deployment at a time.
</Warn>

For the cross-database case the working answer at the time of writing is community
tooling rather than graph-node itself.
[subgraph-migration](https://github.com/vincenttaglia/subgraph-migration) exists for
exactly this, and its interesting design choice is that it streams `pg_dump` into
`pg_restore` over SSH, so you do not need a disk anywhere large enough to hold the
dump. That constraint is the one that catches people planning a migration on paper:
the intermediate file is frequently larger than any spare volume you have.

<Model>
Ask of any capacity decision not just "can I change this later" but "what does
changing it cost on each side of the boundary". Here the boundary is one database.
Inside it, a supported command. Outside it, a project.
</Model>

## What to build before you scale.

In order, and the order matters:

1. **POI age alerting** per allocation, warning well before
   <Param name="max_poi_staleness_days" />.
2. **Deployment health**, distinguishing syncing, synced and failed. A failed
   subgraph is invisible in aggregate metrics.
3. **Payment pipeline monitoring.** Unaggregated value and unredeemed RAV age.
4. **POI divergence checking** against other indexers, before a dispute rather than
   after.
5. **Postgres backups you have actually restored from.** An untested backup is a
   belief, not a backup.
6. **A shard layout you have thought about**, and a written answer to where a
   deployment goes when the current database fills. Deciding that under pressure is
   how an operator discovers the boundary above at the worst moment.

Only then add another chain.

## On running it alone.

Worth saying plainly, since most material skips it. Chains do not pause for holidays,
illness or a bad week. An indexer with one operator has a single point of failure who
needs to sleep.

Options exist: an arrangement with another operator to watch each other's alerts,
automation that closes or reallocates on failure, running a smaller allocation set
that degrades gracefully. Any of these is better than intending to be vigilant.
