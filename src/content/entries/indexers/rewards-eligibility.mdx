Allocating stake and submitting a valid POI used to be the whole of what indexing
rewards required. Since GIP-0079 it is not. There is a second gate, asserted by an
oracle, and an indexer who fails it earns nothing on allocations that are otherwise
perfectly healthy.

The argument for the change was that rewards were reaching indexers who were not
serving anybody. The gate is the answer to that: to collect issuance you must be
demonstrably answering real queries from real users.

## The criterion, in full.

It is one sentence, and it is worth reading slowly because almost every
misunderstanding comes from reading it fast.

You must be **active on <Param name="reo_active_days_required"/>** within a rolling
window of <Param name="reo_evaluation_window_days"/>. A day counts as active if you
served **at least one qualifying query** that day. A query qualifies only if it
satisfies all three of these at once, on the same query:

<Flow steps={["HTTP 200 OK", "fast enough", "near chainhead"]} sep="+" />

- The response status was **200 OK**.
- The response took less than <Param name="reo_query_latency_ms"/>.
- The data served was less than <Param name="reo_query_blocks_behind"/> behind
  chainhead.

A query that returns quickly but from a deployment three hundred thousand blocks
behind is not a qualifying query. Neither is a fresh, correct answer that took six
seconds. The three conditions are a conjunction, not a score.

## What it is not.

This is the part people get wrong, and the error is expensive because it is an error
in the direction of complacency.

**It is not a rate.** There is no queries-per-second threshold anywhere in the
criterion, and no percentage of network traffic you have to capture. One qualifying
query on <Param name="reo_active_days_required"/> is sufficient. A million queries a
day on four days is not. The measurement is a daily boolean, and the count of true
days is the only thing that matters.

**It is not an uptime measure.** GIP-0079 describes
<Param name="reo_active_days_required"/> out of
<Param name="reo_evaluation_window_days"/> as roughly 18% uptime and says the initial
thresholds were "intentionally set conservatively". The bar is deliberately low. It
is there to exclude indexers serving nobody, not to rank the ones who are.

**It is not a competition.** Nothing about your eligibility depends on how any other
indexer performed. Every indexer who clears the bar clears it.

<Model>
Read it as a liveness check, not a performance score. It asks whether real query
traffic reached you and came back acceptable, on a handful of separate days. It does
not ask how much, how fast on average, or how you compare.
</Model>

## Where the number actually comes from.

Here is the part that costs people money.

The oracle does not read your indexer. GIP-0079 states that "the initial
implementation of Oracle Nodes builds upon pre-existing data pipelines (from our
geographically distributed gateways into Google BigQuery) and queries that data
directly". The input is the **gateway's** record of what it routed to you and how
that went.

Your own telemetry is a different measurement of a different population. An
indexer-service query counter increments for everything that reaches your endpoint,
including traffic that never came through a gateway and therefore never entered the
pipeline the oracle reads. The two numbers are not the same number, and they can
disagree in the one direction that hurts:

<Flow steps={["your metrics: healthy", "gateway routed you nothing", "oracle: unqualified", "rewards: zero"]} hot={3} />

Two consequences worth internalising.

You cannot self-test eligibility by generating load against your own stack. Traffic
you send yourself is invisible to the oracle by construction.

And a green local dashboard is not evidence of eligibility. If the gateway is not
routing to you, every counter you own can look correct while the criterion is being
failed continuously. The usual reasons the gateway routes you nothing are
[a cost model priced above the field](/indexers/cost-models-and-rules/) and the
gateway's own performance policy, neither of which raises anything on your side.

<Warn title="The instrument determines the answer">
When somebody quotes a figure about your query performance, ask which instrument
produced it. Gateway telemetry knows what was routed and what the user experienced,
and cannot see whether the data was right. Your own metrics know everything that
reached you, and cannot see what never got sent. The oracle uses the first.
</Warn>

## Status, periods and renewal.

The oracle runs daily and publishes to a contract on Arbitrum One. Every registered
indexer has a status from the moment the oracle runs, so the answer to "does my status
only appear after I have been active for
<Param name="reo_active_days_required"/>" is no. Before you qualify you are not blank,
you are **Unqualified**, which is a stated result rather than an absence of one.

The statuses on the live dashboard read as follows.

- **Eligible.** You cleared the bar and are inside a current period.
- **Grace.** Eligible, with the end of the period approaching.
- **Unqualified.** Never renewed, or did not meet the criteria.
- **Expired.** A period ended and was not renewed.

Qualifying does not grant eligibility permanently and does not grant it for a single
day. It grants a **period**, <Param name="reo_eligibility_period_days"/> by default,
and the criteria document is explicit that you keep earning for the whole of that
period even if the criteria change afterwards. The default is worth checking rather
than assuming: the oracle repository states the live value is readable from the
contract.

Because evaluation is daily and the window rolls, there is no cliff to plan around.
There is a continuous requirement that some real traffic reached you recently, and a
buffer of roughly a fortnight behind it.

## Is it actually enforced?

Worth asking rather than assuming, because until recently the answer was no. On
2026-08-20 the deployed Rewards Manager had none of the machinery to act on a verdict:
`getProviderEligibilityOracle()` reverted, along with every other function GIP-0086 adds.
The oracle published and nothing consumed it.

That changed at some point in the eleven days after. Read from Arbitrum One at block
500226907 on 2026-08-31, the Rewards Manager at
`0x971B9d3d0Ae3ECa029CAB5eA1fB0F72c85e6a525` answers:

An oracle address is set, a reclaim address is set, and `getRevertOnIneligible()` is
`true`. That last one is the operationally interesting flag: the contract is configured to
**revert** on an ineligible indexer rather than quietly return zero. A reverting
transaction is at least a loud failure, which is a mercy on a site whose recurring theme is
that nothing here fails loudly.

The 2026-08-20 reading is [graph-support #23](https://github.com/nightswatchhq/graph-support/issues/23),
which carries the reverting calls in full. Neither this page nor that issue has established
the block at which the upgrade landed.

Two honest limits on the current reading. It is a point in time, and these values are governable,
so re-run the calls rather than trusting this paragraph. And a configured flag is evidence
that the path exists, not a measurement of it firing on a real ineligible indexer, which is
not something this page has observed.

## Where the trust sits.

Eligibility is an **oracle assertion**, and that puts it in a different category from
everything else that determines what you are paid.

A POI is a claim you make, and the protocol's answer to a wrong claim is a dispute
with staked capital behind it. An eligibility verdict is not a claim you make. It is
an assertion about you, derived from data you do not hold, published by an oracle
appointed through governance. There is no arbitration path for it in the way there is
for [a disputed POI](/indexers/disputes-and-slashing/).

That is not an accusation. Some facts genuinely cannot be computed on chain and this
is one of them. But it does mean the recourse for a wrong verdict is
[governance rather than adjudication](/ecosystem/oracles-and-observability/), and it
is worth knowing which of the two you are in before you need one.

## What to monitor.

The failure is silent in the usual way. Allocations stay open, POIs keep being
accepted, nothing errors, and the rewards are simply not there.

- **Your status on the oracle, directly.** It is the only measurement that counts, and
  it is published. Do not infer it from your own metrics.
- **Whether the gateway is routing to you at all**, per deployment. Zero routed
  traffic is the actual failure mode behind most ineligibility, and it looks exactly
  like an absence of demand.
- **Latency and chainhead distance as a pair.** Each is fine alone and the criterion
  needs both at once. A deployment falling behind stops producing qualifying queries
  while continuing to answer every one of them with a 200.

<Reveal question="Before reading on: why does the criterion count days rather than queries?">
Because a query count is trivially gameable and a day count is not, at least not
cheaply.

Query volume can be manufactured. If the bar were a total, the cheapest way to clear
it would be to arrange for the traffic rather than to earn it. Spreading the
requirement across separate days changes what is being tested: not "did enough queries
happen" but "were you a working member of the network on several distinct occasions".

It also makes the bar honest about what it can see. The oracle is reading routing
records, and routing is not under your control. A volume threshold would punish a
well-run indexer that the gateway happened not to favour that week. A handful of
separate days asks only that you were there and answering when something did arrive,
which is the most the instrument can actually support.
</Reveal>
